### Introduction – “The Rise of the Art-Novel and the Question of Class”

**1. Defining the “art-novel” / modernist novel**

- McGurl starts from T. S. Eliot’s intro to Djuna Barnes’s _Nightwood_ and the idea that only “sensibilities trained on poetry” can appreciate it. That’s his example of a **novel conceived explicitly as “art”** and not as general entertainment. 
    
- He links this to Henry James (and before him Flaubert): James sets out to reclaim the Anglo-American novel from mere “pudding”-like popular fiction and insist it should count as _fine art_ and confer prestige on both author and reader. 
    
- The basic definition that underwrites the whole book: **the modernist novel is the novel conceived as art and as a bearer of cultural capital** – it wants to be in the same symbolic space as painting, music, etc., not just circulating library fodder. 
    

**2. The novel, mass literacy, and “product differentiation”**

- With mass schooling and cheap print, “almost everyone” is reading novels; James worries his genre is swamped by an “abysmally absorbent” public of millions whose taste is only an instinct. 
    
- The art-novel emerges **inside** this mass market, not outside it: it’s a way of producing distinctions within a field flooded by mediocre fiction, analogous to how mass-produced black Model Ts give way to a hierarchy of more “distinctive” car models in the 1920s. 
    
- So “modernism vs mass culture” isn’t a clean Great Divide; modernism is in a **dialectical relation** with mass culture, rejecting it rhetorically while relying on its institutions and audiences. 
    

**3. Pastoral, primitivism, and “the low”**

- Using William Empson’s concept of **pastoral**, McGurl argues modernist novels often “put the complex into the simple”: sophisticated, urban, highly educated writers represent “simple” or “low” lives (the child, the idiot, the primitive, the folk, etc.) for an educated audience. 
    
- This means American modernism stands in a **“pastoral” relation to American mass culture**, and the art-novel stands in a pastoral relation to the novel as a low-status genre. The presence of the naive, stupid, or “simple” becomes the necessary foil that lets the “complex” (writer and reader) feel their own sophistication – Nietzsche’s “pathos of distance.” 
    

**4. Mental labor and the professional-managerial class**

- Modernism is linked to what Richard Poirier calls **“grim reading”**: texts that intimidate and demand work rather than offer easy pleasure. But McGurl says it’s not just anti-pleasure; it also reflects a new ideology where **work itself – especially mental work – is a site of pleasure and value.**
    
- This plugs into the rise of the **professional-managerial class** in the U.S.: a large “white-collar mass” of people who handle symbols and information (lawyers, managers, teachers, advertisers, writers, etc.). The art-novel participates in the _internal_ differentiation of this class, not just in a simple bourgeois-versus-workers opposition. 
    
- Crucially, modernist fiction **shifts the axis of value from moral virtue to intellectual virtue (“smarts”)** – pushing back against the older, Howells-type realist idea that novels should express the respectable virtues of the middle class. 
    

**5. From “difference” to “distinction”: his method**

- ==McGurl calls his approach **“methodological philistinism”**: he brackets questions of aesthetic greatness and instead treats modernist novels as social objects that produce distinctions (a bit like a sociologist studying religion without deciding which god is real).== 
    
- Draws on Veblen, Weber, Mannheim, and especially Bourdieu’s idea of **“classification struggle”**: modernist novels don’t just depict social classes – they actively participate in struggles over what counts as high/low, smart/stupid, refined/vulgar. 
    
- He admits this is still an _immanent_ critique – it still takes modernist literature seriously and finds it “terribly interesting” – but it tries to keep some distance from its own temptation to aesthetic worship. 
    

In short: the intro sets up the **art-novel** as a modernist form that, within mass culture, turns the novel into a high-art object, tightly bound up with class, mental labor, and the production of social distinction – and announces a sociological, Bourdieu-inflected method for studying that.

### Chapter 1 – “The Mind’s Eye and Mental Labor: Forms of Distinction in the Fiction of Henry James”

Chapter 1 is where he shows how all that plays out in **Henry James’s** work.

#### 1. “The Novel as Masterpiece”

- McGurl opens with _The Golden Bowl_: Charlotte and Amerigo in the little Bloomsbury shop, the “master” dealer, and the “conspicuous” golden bowl that’s “simple but singularly elegant.” 
    
- The **golden bowl** is read as a symbol of the **novel as art-object** – a “well-wrought urn” that stands out in the marketplace and even gives its name to the book. The shop, the glass doors, the “master,” and the bowl together allegorise the modern literary market and the high-art novel’s place in it. 
    
- He connects this to Debray on the **codex as sacred object**: just as the Book becomes fetishized in religions of the Book, the printed novel and James’s New York Edition (with Coburn’s photos) ritualise access to a special “house of fiction.” 
    
- Coburn’s frontispiece photographs show **shut doors and thresholds**, keeping us outside the “house of fiction.” The key question becomes **who gets in?** The high-art novel divides readers into those who have the cultural/interpretive key and those who don’t. 
    

So James’s late style and book design make the novel itself look like a **masterpiece to be owned, entered, or admired**, reinforcing its role as cultural capital.

#### 2. “Epistemologies of Social Class”

- McGurl then turns to James’s role in the romance/realism debate, drawing on Michael McKeon vs Ian Watt. The point: the novel as a genre is produced by a **dialectic of romance and history**, not by realism simply replacing romance. 
    
- In James’s _The American_ and its preface, questions of **truth and virtue** are bound up with **class**: the naive American businessman vs old European aristocracy. The epistemological question (“how do we know what’s true?”) and the class question (“who belongs where?”) are entangled. 
    
- Crucially, James’s fiction begins to shift emphasis from **moral virtue** (good heart, industriousness) toward **intellectual subtlety** and interpretive finesse as the mark of distinction – a move that will characterize the art-novel more generally. 
    

#### 3. “The Romance of Romance: Virtue Unrewarded”

- Here McGurl reads James’s revisiting of romance conventions, especially the idea that virtue should be rewarded (the good hero gets the girl/estate, etc.).
    
- In works like _The American_, **moral virtue is not rewarded** in any straightforward way: the decent middle-class protagonist does _not_ triumph over the aristocrats. Middle-class uplift narratives falter. 
    
- This allows James to detach value from simple bourgeois morality and reattach it to more abstract forms of distinction (taste, perception, intelligence) – preparing the ground for the high-art novel whose “reward” is mostly **symbolic capital**.
    

#### 4. “Divisive Perspectivism” – the “mind’s eye” and readerly labor

- McGurl then zooms in on James’s famous narrative technique: the **center of consciousness** / limited point of view (“the mind’s eye”). Characters like Amerigo are shown carefully reading other people’s bodies and gestures, converting what they see into nuanced mental interpretations. 
    
- In _The Golden Bowl_, when Charlotte enters Fanny Assingham’s parlor and pointedly speaks to Fanny rather than Amerigo, Amerigo reads this tiny “discrimination” as a flood of meaning; her silent body becomes **raw material for interpretive labor**. 
    
- This dramatises a key process: **visible things are turned into intellectual property** by an active, discriminating mind – mirroring how James the author turns the “stuff” of the social world into refined narrative form. 
    
- At the same time, the difficulty of James’s style (famously tortuous sentences) requires strenuous mental work from readers. Being a reader of James (a “Jamesian”) becomes itself a **mark of intellectual distinction**, a way to distinguish oneself from the “ordinary reader” of ordinary novels. 
    

So James’s perspectivism is **divisive**: it both _represents_ class/intellectual hierarchies and _produces_ a hierarchy among readers, separating those who can follow the intricate “mind’s eye” from those who bounce off.

### Big picture: what Ch. 1 is doing for the book

Put simply:

- The **Introduction** lays out a general theory: the modernist art-novel is a novel that wants to be fine art, bound up with mass culture, mental labor, and class distinction.
    
- **Chapter 1** shows **Henry James** as the key early figure in this process:
    
    - he models the novel as **masterpiece / sacred object**,
        
    - reworks class and romance so that **intellectual subtlety becomes the main axis of value**,
        
    - and develops narrative forms (the “mind’s eye,” difficult syntax, restricted POV) that enact and demand **mental labor**, thereby turning both the novel and its readers into sites of social distinction.
