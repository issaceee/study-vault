# Essay Development Plan — Prelims Dissection, Summer 2026

Built from a full read of the Prelims marks, tutorial essays and marker comments across the year. Reference this for the next two years.

---

## THE CORE DIAGNOSIS — going deeper

The relay-structure problem (stacking theories A→B→C, each patching the last one's gap) isn't the root issue. It's one visible symptom of something that shows up across almost every essay in this collection, including ones that scored very well. Tracing it required looking past the Prelims pair and back through the whole year.

**The pattern: your prose repeatedly treats *comprehensiveness* as the source of authority for a claim, rather than trusting a narrower, sharper, more selective claim to carry the weight on its own.** Comprehensive theoretical apparatus, comprehensive coverage of a question's implications, comprehensive synthesis of existing scholarship, comprehensive fairness between two compared texts, comprehensive resolution at the end — all of these are different costumes for the same move: reaching for more ground covered as the proof of rigor, when your markers keep telling you the actual proof of rigor is a precise, independently-held claim that doesn't need to cover everything to be right.

Here's the evidence, pulled from essays that otherwise scored very differently from each other:

- **Beowulf manuscript essay:** "you deftly synthesise critical arguments that range across paleography, codicology, etc. to make your case... You can afford to try and offer your own readings, though!" — praised for the breadth of synthesis, then told directly that the breadth was substituting for your own independent claim.
- **Stein essay:** "I would encourage you to begin to make more abstract claims about the secondary literature... scholars tend to see aesthetic value in *The Autobiography* as... Being able to make these broad assessments will help you articulate your intervention." Same note again — more synthesis isn't what's missing; a clearer sense of where *you* stand apart from the field is.
- **Howards End:** "It's not entirely clear to me how your argument here is all that different from Outka's." You'd built a genuinely strong reading, but hadn't cut the cord from the scholar you were extending.
- **Kipling ("White Man's Burden"):** three theoretical lenses used; tutor asks "did you really need this [third] section? Why not just two methods?" — an extra framework added for coverage, not because it was doing independent work.
- **Chrighton Abbey ("All was told"):** "your claim that the story utilizes three techniques seems arbitrary. Why three techniques and not five? or two?" — the same instinct toward comprehensive-looking structure, called out directly as arbitrary rather than argued.
- **The Kipling essay's opening, and the "Story" (OED) essay's opening:** both dinged for spending too long comprehensively unpacking the question itself before arguing — "bogged down… I don't have a clear suggestion here — I just felt you could have been breezier, or less neurotic" is the tutor's word for it, not mine, but it's the same reflex applied to the prompt instead of to theory.
- **The 1830-1910 marked paper, Q10b:** "Extraordinary length for exam essay – almost 2700 words" against a ~2000-word form. A literal, non-metaphorical version of the same impulse: more coverage read as more safety.
- **Every essay in the file ends on a tidy synthesis** — "thus," "overall," a claim that reconciles everything raised. Not one essay in the whole collection ends on a genuine unresolved tension.
- **Every comparative essay in the file gives both texts roughly equal, parallel treatment** (Rossetti/Braddon, Melville/James, Poe/Doyle/Douglass, Joyce/Beckett) — comprehensiveness of fairness, never one reading allowed to dominate or distort the other on purpose.
- **Hedges** ("it can be argued," "seems to," "perhaps," "in this sense") recur constantly — each one is a small comprehensiveness move at the sentence level: qualifying a claim so it doesn't have to stand alone as fully proven.
- **Your best line is usually in the conclusion, not the introduction** — meaning the fully independent, unhedged, confident version of your claim only gets stated once everything else has been comprehensively built up around it. The relay structure is really the same thing at essay-scale: you let yourself use a theory decisively only after you've shown you've covered its whole justification first.

Once you see it this way, the relay-structure fix I gave you earlier ("cap yourself at two frameworks") is really just the theory-specific version of a much bigger habit: **write the sharp, selective, independent claim first, and only add coverage where it's actually doing new work — not as insurance.**

### What this does NOT explain — separate issues worth naming honestly

Not everything in this collection reduces to the pattern above. Two things are genuinely distinct and shouldn't be folded in:

- **Mechanical execution under time pressure:** "some typos," "clumsy presentation," "rushed and imprecise," "messy, rushed" recur specifically on essays written in exam conditions, not portfolio conditions (compare the Paper 1a/1b essays, written over 10 days, which have none of these O&P complaints, against the Prelims essays, which have several). This is a pacing/practice issue, not a scholarly-disposition issue — it needs timed past-paper drilling, not a change in how you think about argument.
- **The Early Medieval rubric penalty:** repeating the Grendel example between your commentary and Q11 wasn't about comprehensiveness of argument — it was a rubric-awareness gap. Distinct, already fixed by knowing the rule now.

## 1. WHILE READING THE TEXT (before touching secondary sources)

- Do a first pass with no theory in mind. Mark every moment where the primary text itself does something formally strange: a repetition, a genre shift, a syntactic oddity, an image that doesn't fit, a word doing double duty. This is your evidence bank.
- Your strongest essays (Beowulf manuscript, Howards End, the Kipling circus ad) start from something *specific and textual* and reach for theory to explain it — not the reverse. Text first, always, even with ten minutes on the clock: spend most of that time finding three or four textual moments, not skimming theory notes.
- Ask yourself, honestly, before you even open a secondary source: *what is the smallest, most specific claim this textual moment could support on its own, without any theory behind it yet?* Write that sentence down. It's often close to your real thesis. Theory should sharpen it, not replace it.

## 2. WHILE WRITING

- Before drafting, write each framework you plan to use as a single line: theory A does X, theory B does Y. Check whether each targets a genuinely *different angle* on the same textual problem, or whether B only exists to patch A's hole. If B is patching A, rewrite the plan until each framework has independent work to do (model: Hegglund sees false purity, Outka sees temporal management — two angles on the same house, not a relay).
- Hard rule while drafting: any paragraph that introduces or explains a theorist must end by touching the primary text. Not "get back to it eventually" — that same paragraph. If you're still explaining the same theorist two paragraphs in, stop and cut to the text.
- Don't bury your best line. Check your conclusion's core claim against your introduction's — if the conclusion is sharper (a real pattern here), that's usually your actual thesis, arrived at only once you'd "earned" it through the rest of the essay. Practice trusting it enough to open with it instead.
- Avoid rigid enumerated openings ("this essay highlights three main techniques..."). It's not just "uncharismatic" — it's the comprehensiveness reflex showing up as a locked structure before the argument has had a chance to develop organically. Let the number of moving parts emerge from the argument, not be announced as a checklist up front.
- Watch the boundary between your reading and the scholar's. If a scholar's argument is doing most of the interpretive work and you're mainly paraphrasing it well, say explicitly, in your own sentence, what you're doing that they aren't (flagged on Joyce/Beckett, Howards End, and implicitly on Beowulf and Stein too — this is the single most repeated note in the whole file).
- When you're tempted to answer the question comprehensively — covering every implication of its terms before you argue anything — stop and ask whether a faster, more selective gloss of the question would let you get to your claim two paragraphs sooner. Test this against the "neurotic" comment: it wasn't wrong content, it was unnecessary throat-clearing.

## 3. ONCE THE ESSAY IS DRAFTED (5-minute self-check)

- Reread and mark every paragraph T (theory-explaining) or R (reading the text) in the margin. Two T's in a row is a flag — go back and cut or fuse.
- Scan your own transitions for the shape "X diagnoses [problem], but cannot Y, so we turn to Z." Not banned — but when you see it, check: is Z about to do its own independent work on the text, or just inherit A's leftover problem?
- Count your hedges per page. Try rewriting one paragraph with them stripped out. If the claim survives being said plainly, that's the voice to train toward. If it collapses without the hedge, the argument wasn't fully made yet — fix the argument, not the hedge.
- Check whether the essay resolves too neatly. If every paragraph builds toward a clean synthesizing "thus," ask whether that's honest to the material or a reflex toward closure. Consider, deliberately, cutting your last paragraph's second half.
- Check your word count against the form's stated range. If you're significantly over, ask which section is over-covering rather than arguing — the 2700-word Q10b essay is the cautionary example.

## 4. GETTING FEEDBACK

- Ask a sharper question than "how did I do." Ask directly: *"In this essay, is each framework doing separate work on the text, or is one bailing out the other?"* and, at least once a term, the bigger version of that question: *"Where in this essay am I covering ground instead of making a claim?"* That second question is the one that actually targets the root pattern, not just its theory-shaped symptom.
- If you try the overreading technique (see below), ask specifically: *"Did the diagnostic turn at the end actually land, or did the overreading just read as a mistake?"*

## HOW TO BUILD THE CAPACITY TO MAKE AN ORIGINAL CLAIM ABOUT A FIELD

This isn't a trait you either have or don't — it's already visible, inconsistently, across your own essays. The question is how to make it reliable.

**Evidence you already do this:** Quicksand ("Much scholarship echoes this view... Nevertheless, while Roos reads Quicksand through the Ariadne myth... I argue that the novel's ending ultimately resists a tragic and mythic coherence that such a framework assumes") and Howards End (Hegglund vs. Outka, positioned against each other) both do it well. Quicksand in particular is the last essay in the file — direct evidence the skill grew over the year, unprompted, after the Stein tutor's note asking for exactly this kind of sentence.

**The mechanism, made explicit — two steps, every time:**
1. Steelman the existing reading precisely — name the scholar, state their strongest version, not a vague "some critics say."
2. Find the one specific textual detail the existing reading can't metabolize, and use that friction point — not a general dissatisfaction — as the pivot into your own claim. (Quicksand's pivot: Roos reads the ending as tragic sacrifice; the text actually ends on biological repetition — a detail the sacrifice-frame has no room for.)

**Concrete practices — pick two or three, not all at once:**

1. **10-minute field map before writing.** For any set text: 3–4 lines, "critics tend to read this as ___," one line per position, even guessed from lecture framing. Originality is only visible against a mapped terrain — without the map you can't tell if a reading is new or just unlabeled.
2. **Keep a running friction log, separate from any essay.** Whenever a secondary source triggers "wait, but what about—", write the unfinished sentence down immediately. This is literally how the Quicksand pivot and the "one race of men" aside in Bulosan happened — the skill is catching the noticing, not inventing it.
3. **Steelman before you pivot, explicitly.** Write "the strongest version of [scholar]'s reading is ___" before writing your own claim. Weak original claims usually come from a strawmanned existing reading. The Howards End note ("not entirely clear how different from Outka's") suggests the steelmanning step got skipped there.
4. **Practice on pairs of scholars who disagree with each other**, not just one scholar you're extending. Locating a third position between two existing ones (Hegglund/Outka; the Kipling three lenses) is a smaller creative leap than generating a claim from nothing.
5. **Redefine "original" as "precise," not "unprecedented."** Nearly every strong critical claim is a recombination — a detail from one register (form, archive, a minor character) unsettling a reading built on another (theme, plot, a major character). Kapwa answering what Chuang-Tzu couldn't; the "sleep" reading against Roos's "circularity" — both small, specific recombinations, not invention from scratch.
6. **Do it as a standalone drill sometimes, no essay attached.** Once a fortnight: field-map guesses plus one friction sentence on a text you already know, zero stakes. The instinct fires more reliably without exam pressure and juggling frameworks at once (Quicksand, Howards End were both lower-pressure contexts than the Prelims essays) — practicing it in isolation is how it becomes reflexive under pressure later.

## NEW CHALLENGES TO GROW INTO

These four aren't random new skills — each one is a different terrain for practicing the same underlying move the core diagnosis points to: **trusting a narrower, sharper, more selective claim over a comprehensive one.** Reading them this way should make them feel connected rather than like four separate assignments.

### 1. Let an essay end unresolved
Every essay in this collection ends in synthesis. This is the closure-version of the pattern: needing to reconcile everything raised rather than trusting that an honest tension can be the last word. Drill: on a practice essay, instead of writing the synthesizing final paragraph, write the paragraph that names exactly what your argument *can't* account for, and stop there.

### 2. Build from period-specific material, not just modern theorists
This is the evidence-version of the pattern: leaning on the comprehensive, recognized authority of an established theoretical school, instead of trusting a smaller, self-found piece of archival specificity (a review, an ad, an OED date) to carry real interpretive weight on its own. Your strongest historicist moments already do this (the Ringling Brothers ad against Kipling, the Beowulf scribal corrections). Try banning yourself from citing a 20th/21st-century theorist in your first two paragraphs of a future essay — build from primary period material first, bring theory in only to name what you've already found.

### 3. Write a genuinely asymmetrical comparison
This is the fairness-version of the pattern: your comparisons are consistently balanced (Rossetti/Braddon, Melville/James, Poe/Doyle/Douglass, Joyce/Beckett) because equal, comprehensive treatment feels like the safe, defensible choice. The stretch is trusting an unfair, selective, even overreaching reading to be more illuminating than a balanced one — and then owning what it cost.

**How it works:** pick two texts. Let text/framework A dominate the reading of text B completely, past the point of fairness — push every image of B through A's grid even where it visibly doesn't fit. Then, in the essay's second half, turn around and name exactly what got flattened by the forcing, and argue that the flattening itself is diagnostic of something real (often: what a period or a reader *needed* the text to mean).

**Concrete example (Faustus / Genesis):** read *Doctor Faustus* entirely through the Fall — Mephistopheles as pure serpent, the pact-signing as the eating of the fruit, no room for the pact's specific Reformation-era print/contract-law texture or Faustus's specifically academic ambition. Then expose what that flattens, and argue the flattening shows how badly early modern culture wanted to refold new anxieties (science, print, individual ambition) back into an old, safe story.

**Concrete example (Moby-Dick, since you've already written on it):** read the whole novel through Jonah as the single grid — Ishmael's opening restlessness as pure Jonah-flight, "Cetology" forced into typology, the doubloon as golden calf — flattening your own earlier point about the novel's insistence on multiplicity. Then turn and name what that violence crushed (the comedy, the encyclopedic mania, the flat refusal of the epilogue to moralize), and argue the overreach itself reenacts mid-19th-century American Protestant anxiety about a science it couldn't yet absorb.

**Real secondary-source model of this move:** Freud's "The Uncanny" on Hoffmann's "The Sandman" — reads the whole story through castration anxiety, subordinating its games with narrative unreliability and madness to one grid. Hélène Cixous's response ("Fiction and Its Phantoms") argues Freud's reading flattens exactly what makes the story literary — and that what Freud's reading had to suppress is itself diagnostic of psychoanalysis's need for mastery. The overreading and the essay diagnosing its blind spot are more interesting together than either alone.

**On marks — this will not read as a mistake if handled right:**
- State the method as your actual thesis in paragraph one: *"I want to show what happens when [framework] is applied to [text] with its own violence intact, because that violence is evidence of [X]."* Don't let the diagnostic turn arrive as a surprise coda — announce it as the plan from the start.
- Ratio matters. In a **practice/tutorial essay**: go as far as 70% overreading / 30% diagnostic — the point is stretching the muscle, and your tutor can tell you if the turn lands.
- In an **exam essay**: flip it to roughly 40% staged overreading / 60% diagnostic — under time pressure with no one to sanity-check you, the self-aware payoff needs to dominate the word count so a marker can't miss that the essay knows exactly what it's doing.
- Your existing instinct for honest self-critique (the "one race of men" aside in the Bulosan essay, your most trustworthy moment in it) is the exact tool that makes this safe. You're not asking permission to overreach — you're staging it and then doing what you already do well: catching yourself.

### 4. Cut the hedges, let a sentence be true
This is the sentence-level version of the pattern, and probably the easiest one to practice daily since it doesn't require planning a whole essay differently. "It can be argued," "seems to," "perhaps," "in this sense" recur constantly as a way of letting a claim stand without it having to be fully, independently true on its own. Your actual best sentences have zero hedging ("They are not what the living voice refers to, but what it *is*") — that's part of why they land as WOW moments. Drill: after finishing an essay, count hedge phrases per page, then rewrite one paragraph with all of them stripped. If the claim survives being said plainly, that's the voice to train toward.

## QUICK-REFERENCE CHECKLIST FOR ANY FUTURE ESSAY

- [ ] Root check: is there a place in this essay where I reached for more coverage — a third framework, a fuller synthesis, a fairer comparison, a tidier ending — because it felt safer than a narrower claim, rather than because it was doing genuinely new work?
- [ ] Did I find the textual weirdness before I reached for theory?
- [ ] Does every framework I'm using have independent work to do, or is one just patching another's gap?
- [ ] After naming a theory's limit, did I use the next theory immediately — or spend a paragraph explaining it first?
- [ ] Is my sharpest claim in the introduction, or hiding in the conclusion?
- [ ] Did I avoid a rigid enumerated thesis ("this essay shows three things")?
- [ ] Have I said explicitly what my reading does that my cited scholars don't?
- [ ] T/R margin check: any two theory-paragraphs in a row?
- [ ] Hedge count: would my key claims survive being said without "perhaps" or "it can be argued"?
- [ ] Does the ending earn its synthesis, or is it reflexive closure?
- [ ] Word count check: is any section over-length because it's covering rather than arguing?
