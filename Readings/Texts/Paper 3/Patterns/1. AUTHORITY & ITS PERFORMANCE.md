## OVERARCHING FRAME

- Victorian literature emerges in a moment when traditional sources of authority — religion, inherited hierarchy, classical education — are under sustained strain.  
- As these frameworks weaken, literature increasingly assumes the task of producing authority in their absence.  
- Authority is no longer guaranteed by position or tradition; it must be *constructed*, *performed*, and *circulated* through literary form.

==> Authority becomes a formal and ethical problem, not merely a thematic one.

---

## ROMANTICISM (Inherited Authority of the Self)

- Romanticism bequeaths to the Victorian period a model of authority grounded in individual consciousness and expressive sincerity.  
- The writer claims legitimacy through depth of feeling, intensity of perception, and the authenticity of inner experience.  
- Authority here is expressive rather than institutional: truth is validated by inwardness.

==> Authority is located in *subjective depth*.

## REALISM (Mid-Victorian Consolidation of Authority)

- Realism emerges in response to the insufficiency of Romantic inwardness for a socially complex, industrial world.  
- Authority is relocated from individual sensibility to social knowledge.  
- The realist novel presents itself as capable of knowing society more fully than any single consciousness within it.

==> Authority shifts from *felt truth* to *represented reality*.

### Core primary texts (you have read)
- **George Eliot, *Middlemarch***  
- **Elizabeth Gaskell, *North and South***  
- **Charles Dickens, *Bleak House***  

### How authority is staged formally
- Omniscient or reflective narration produces a surplus of knowledge unavailable to characters.  
- Narrative voice blends sympathy with judgement, modelling moral authority.  
- Structural breadth (multiple plots, social networks) substitutes for religious certainty.

### Close-reading pressure points
- Eliot’s narratorial generalisations and reflective pauses.  
- Dickens’s split narration (*Bleak House*): Esther’s humility vs the novel’s epistemic authority.  
- Explanatory syntax that asserts understanding through qualification.

==> Authority is *narratively manufactured*, not naturally possessed.

### Suggested extensions

**Less-canonical**
- Elizabeth Gaskell, *Mary Barton* (working-class mediation)

## REFORM, BENEVOLENCE, AND ASYMMETRICAL AUTHORITY

- Reformist Victorian literature often presents itself as speaking *for* the marginalised.  
- Sympathy becomes the dominant ethical mode, substituting for direct agency.  
- Authority is benevolent but unevenly distributed.

==> To speak for others is already to exercise power.

### Core primary texts (you have read)
- **Elizabeth Barrett Browning, “The Cry of the Children”**  
- **Margaret Fuller, “Our City Charities”**  
- **Gaskell, *North and South*** (workers mediated through middle-class consciousness)

### How to read closely
- Examine who speaks, who is represented, and who is ventriloquised.  
- Track moments where moral urgency replaces structural change.

**Less-canonical**

## SLAVERY, TESTIMONY, AND COUNTER-AUTHORITY

- Abolitionist and Black Atlantic texts disrupt Victorian assumptions about who is authorised to speak.  
- Authority is claimed not through omniscience, but through lived experience and testimony.  
- These texts expose the limits of dominant literary forms in representing racialised suffering.

==> Authority shifts from representation to *witness*.

### Core primary texts (you have read)
- **Frederick Douglass, *Narrative of the Life***  
- **Harriet Beecher Stowe, *Uncle Tom’s Cabin***  
- **Frances Ellen Watkins Harper, abolitionist poetry**  
- **W.E.B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*** (“Sorrow Songs”)

### How authority operates differently
- Douglass asserts authority through first-person testimony and rhetorical self-fashioning.  
- Du Bois explicitly frames Black expressive culture as both voiced and systematically unheard.  
- The “Sorrow Songs” articulate a form of expression that exceeds written literary authority.

==> Literature can speak the unspoken — but only partially.

### Suggested extensions
**Seminal**
- Olaudah Equiano, *Interesting Narrative*  
**Less-canonical**
- Harriet Jacobs, *Incidents in the Life of a Slave Girl*

### Secondary sockets
> Brodhead on circulation and slave narrative  
> Gould on the slave narrative as genre  
> (Optional later) Hartman on silence and archival violence

## EMPIRE AND IMPERIAL AUTHORITY

- Late Victorian literature frequently stages imperial authority as rational, civilising, and domesticating.  
- Genres such as detective fiction promise mastery, logic, and epistemic control.

==> Authority appears as deduction and closure.

### Core primary texts (you have read)
- **Arthur Conan Doyle, “The Speckled Band”**  
- **Rudyard Kipling, “The White Man’s Burden”**

### How authority fractures
- Detective logic reassures, but relies on imperial misdirection.  
- Empire is framed as external threat, even as violence originates within the domestic sphere.  
- Authority leaks where it claims mastery.

==> Imperial authority is performed, not secured.

### Suggested extensions
**Seminal**
- Rider Haggard, *King Solomon’s Mines*  
**Less-canonical**
- Flora Annie Steel (imperial domesticity)

### Secondary sockets
> Siddiqi on empire and repression  
> Bratlinger on Kipling and imperial ideology

## AESTHETICISM (Reorientation of Authority)

- Aestheticism rejects the demand that literature justify itself through truth, morality, or reform.  
- Authority is relocated from knowledge to perception — from explanation to intensity.

==> Authority becomes aesthetic rather than epistemological.

### Core primary texts (you have read)
- **Walter Pater, *The Renaissance***  
- **Oscar Wilde, “The Critic as Artist”**  
- **Wilde, Preface to *The Picture of Dorian Gray***

### How to read closely
- Pater’s cumulative, deferring syntax.  
- Wilde’s epigrammatic authority.  
- Rejection of didactic seriousness.

### Suggested extensions
**Seminal**
- Pater, “Conclusion” (revisited closely)  
**Less-canonical**
- Michael Field (aesthetic authority and gender)

### Secondary sockets

## DECADENCE / FIN DE SIÈCLE

- Decadence exposes authority as exhausted, corrupt, or theatrical.  
- Unity fractures; coherence gives way to excess and ornament.

==> Authority persists only as self-conscious artifice.

### Core primary texts (you have read)
- **Wilde, *The Picture of Dorian Gray***  
- **Arthur Symons, “The Decadent Movement in Literature”**

### How to read closely
- Jewelled style and overload.  
- Fragmentation and autonomy of language.

## SYNTHETIC INSIGHT (EXAM-READY)

- Romanticism grounds authority in feeling.  
- Realism grounds authority in representation.  
- Abolitionist and imperial texts expose authority as uneven and exclusionary.  
- Aestheticism relocates authority into perception.  
- Decadence reveals authority as artifice.

==> Victorian literature interrogates not just *what* authority is, but *who is permitted to speak*, *for whom*, and *with what limits*.
