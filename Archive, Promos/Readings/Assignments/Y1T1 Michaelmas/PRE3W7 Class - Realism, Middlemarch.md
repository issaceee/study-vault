1. In class we'll spend a lot of time talking about Chapter XX, when Dorothea is miserable on her honeymoon. Why is this the emotional and philosophical crux of the novel? Why is Dorothea miserable? Why did she marry Casaubon in the first place? What is so bad about Casaubon? Why does the novel start with Dorothea and Sir James's courtship? 
	- Chapter XX features the first time Dorothea feels that her marriage is wrong, an inkling that her imaginary, ideal future (a intellectual and religious vocation) is replaced by her real future (frustration with Casaubon's emotional distance and possible futility of his scholarship). It is where she realises that not only is this her future reality, but that she might have misjudged everything and there is no way back. It is interesting that she can't really explain it: "had no distinctly shapen grievance that she could state even to herself". I believe that this chapter stages the conflict that defines *Middlemarch* as well as Eliot's realist project: How do people come to realise reality is not what they imagined? That is, it highlights how characters come into them selves as 'real' people in a 'real' world, rather than just characters in a story.
	- I feel that Casaubon represents a sort of failed "transcendence", taking the terms from Catherine Gallagher's essay "George Eliot: Immanent Victorian". A life with him is an imagined religious intellectual life, but in actuality a sterile, detached life that seems to hollow out Dorothea's real, lived experience. It is this realisation that perhaps hints at Dorothea coming into being – that she wants a life for herself – a realisation that arises out of this emotional and philosophical moment in the novel: "she was now in an interval when the very force of her nature heightened its confusion."


	- The emotional conflict Dorothea faces is a representation of a conflict between her idealistic self, her dream of her ideal self and life, and that of reality. It serves to highlight a point in one's life where they must wrestle with the 'ordinariness' of their selves, highlighting the limits that once faces in trying to pursue the ideal life – that of the injusticies, cruelties or merely coincidences of the realistic world.
	- The ideal life that Dorothea imagines herself was one where she is able to reconcile her idealistic, religious role and sentiment of a woman and a wife with that of an intellectual. She wished to serve Casaubon as an equal in that regard, that is, as an assistant, rather than a mere wife. Yet she is undercut by his emotional distance, and the fear of futility of his intellectual pursuits.

2.  We'll also discuss Lydgate's experience in Middlemarch. What do we get from the inclusion of Lydgate in this novel? Why is he one of the main characters? Where does Lydgate go wrong? What does he do that’s so bad? What prevents him from realizing his professional ambitions? Why does he vote for Tyke in Chapter XVIII? Why does he marry Rosamond? What's so terrible about Rosamond?
	- - **Why include Lydgate?**
    
    - He functions as the novel’s figure of modern professional aspiration — scientific, experimental, reformist medicine — set against provincial conservatism.
        
    - He gives Eliot a way of testing _individual ambition vs. social structure_: a man trained in cosmopolitan science placed in a parochial social ecology.
        
    - He is a counterpoint to Casaubon’s sterile erudition: Casaubon’s failure is intellectual sterility; Lydgate’s possible failure is the social naïveté of thinking scientific authority alone will remake institutions and moral economies.
        
- **What Lydgate brings to the novel thematically**
    
    - The novel’s exploration of _limits of agency_: medicine, like morality, is embedded in webs of patronage, credit, reputation. Lydgate discovers that technique and good will cannot by themselves reorder those networks.
        
    - He dramatizes the cost of _aesthetic/ethical isolation_: being right (scientifically) doesn’t equal being able to change entrenched practices without political savvy.
        
    - He helps Eliot examine the tension between _professional vocation_ and _household/domestic economies_ — how public ambitions are compromised by private debts and marriage.
        
- **Where Lydgate goes wrong (specific mistakes)**
    
    - **Social miscalculation**: he underestimates the importance of local alliances and social tact. He treats Middlemarch as if it were a laboratory.
        
    - **Moral blindness in choosing an ally**: he trusts Bulstrode and lets himself be connected to Bulstrode’s respectability; that link costs him moral authority later.
        
    - **Financial imprudence**: borrowing to maintain a genteel household (to sustain Rosamond’s expectations) rather than establishing a sustainable practice; he sacrifices long-term independence for short-term social display.
        
    - **Emotional immaturity** with Rosamond: he marries romantic ideal and impatient passion without fully understanding the domestic demands and the manipulative potential of Rosamond’s temperament.
        
- **Why he votes for Tyke in Chapter XVIII**
    
    - _Practical impatience/communication gap_: Tyke is a local conservative/establishment candidate; Lydgate’s vote indicates either social conformism in petty moments or the complexity of his social positioning — he’s not a pure radical, and this reveals his partial absorption into local modes.
        
    - It shows Lydgate’s incomplete political vision: he is not yet a fully politicised reformer; his professional radicalism doesn’t translate into consistent civic courage.
        
    - The vote is a small sign of the way local pressures and the desire to fit into professional circles pull him away from idealistic purity.
        
- **Why he marries Rosamond; what’s bad about Rosamond**
    
    - He marries Rosamond partly from a desire for domestic sympathy and partly from romantic attraction — he expects a partner who will understand and support his vocation; instead, Rosamond expects social status and consumption.
        
    - Rosamond is not “evil” but **self-interested and socially attuned**: she is anxious about reputational signs, keen on social display, and uninterested in sponsorship of Lydgate’s austere professional ideals.
        
    - The problem is mismatch: Rosamond’s aesthetic (appearance, social ease, consumption) consumes the financial and emotional resources Lydgate needs to establish his reformist practice. She personifies the social values that thwart his project.
        
- **What prevents him from fulfilling his ambitions**
    
    - **Structural constraints** (credit, reputation, patronage) that the novel emphasises repeatedly.
        
    - **Tactical errors** (poor marriage choice, misplaced trust, underestimating social power).
        
    - **Moral compromise** (as his credit and reputation decline, he makes choices that further entangle him financially and morally).
        
    - Eliot’s point: Lydgate is not merely a failed genius — he’s a figure who illustrates how social economy and private desire deform public purpose.

  

3. Why is the Mary Garth-Fred Vincy plotline included in the novel? Focus, perhaps, on the chapter where Fred goes to trade his horse, Chapter XXIII. What's so likeable about Mary Garth? And Caleb Garth? 
- - **Narrative function of their plot**
    
    - Provides a _moral and tonal counterweight_ to the higher-stakes tragedies of Dorothea and Lydgate: practical, domestic virtue vs ambitious tragedy.
        
    - Demonstrates Eliot’s interest in **small-scale moral formation** and the rewards of steady industry and character over dramatic genius or grand projects.
        
    - Balances the novel’s scale: big social critique (Casaubon, Bulstrode) needs scenes of ordinary ethical becoming to show alternative forms of fulfilment.
        
- **Why Fred Vincy matters**
    
    - He embodies _youthful folly and potential redemption_: his path shows one possible way to mature — slow, practical, socially responsible.
        
    - The horse-trading chapter (XXIII) is pivotal for showing the gap between Fred’s fantasy of gentlemanly ease and the reality of economic precariousness; it’s an education-in-failure moment.
        
    - Fred’s stumbling allows Eliot to explore _character-development through consequence_: humiliation and responsibility discipline him.
        
- **Why Mary Garth is likeable / important**
    
    - Mary is the novel’s moral backbone in microcosm: plain, sober, industrious, resistant to social vanity. She’s intelligent without pretension.
        
    - Her worth is practical not performative: she understands labour, economy, decency; she refuses flattery that would compromise her integrity.
        
    - She models Eliot’s ethic: stability, humility, self-discipline — the opposite of the romantic myths that drive Dorothea and Lydgate into crisis.
        
    - Mary’s eventual union with Fred is not romantic fantasy but a mutual reformation: she helps Fred learn responsibility; he provides altered social possibility. The pairing is realistic and morally satisfying.
        
- **Why Caleb Garth matters**
    
    - He’s a figure of _honest labour_ and _moral solidity_: Eliot prizes work as character-producing. Caleb’s competence and principled thrift are a template for social health.
        
    - Caleb’s presence reminds the reader that virtue has material work-basis: reliability, good judgment, trade knowledge. He anchors the novel’s critique of social pretence.
        
    - He’s also a social stabiliser: his calm judgment mediates property disputes, matches, and community trust — the quiet civil virtues the novel values.
        
- **Specifics about Chapter XXIII (horse-trading) and its lessons**
    
    - That episode shows Fred’s interior fantasy vs external economy: he imagines credit and leisure, but is forced to confront actual market exchange and risk.
        
    - The humiliation of failure is formative: it strips Fred of illusions and prepares him to accept work and responsibility. Eliot is teaching that moral education often comes through economic constraint.

   
4. Why is the Bulstrode storyline included in the novel? Focus maybe on Chapter LXI, where we learn his whole backstory.
- - **What Bulstrode represents**
    
    - Religious respectability without moral transparency: a man whose public piety masks a compromised past. He embodies the novel’s concern with _hypocritical respectability_ and the dangers of stained histories in the life of public trust.
        
    - He’s the moral-test case for the link between **past actions** and present social authority: a man of wealth/charity whose hidden deeds make him vulnerable to ruin.
        
    - Through him Eliot explores the ethical relation between reputation and reality: the social world invests trust; when the foundations are shaky, social life can become destructive.
        
- **Why Chapter LXI (his backstory) matters**
    
    - It reveals the origin of Bulstrode’s respectability — how he built himself up and how earlier opportunism, possibly dishonourable acts, lie behind his current status.
        
    - The chapter forces readers to reassess the easy distinction between virtue and vice: Bulstrode’s public beneficence sits beside private moral ambiguity; Eliot complicates moral judgment by showing the causal narrative of his accumulation.
        
    - It provides the moral engine that explains Bulstrode’s later paranoia and moral rigidity: once someone has built on compromise, they fear exposure and thus act more coercively to protect reputation.
        
- **What Bulstrode’s arc does for Eliot’s ethics**
    
    - Eliot uses Bulstrode to argue that _responsibility to truthfulness_ and _acknowledgment of past harms_ are prerequisites for genuine moral authority.
        
    - When Bulstrode attempts concealment, he triggers social harm; Eliot seems to insist that true public trust requires private integrity.
        
    - Bulstrode’s fall is a social lesson: social institutions cannot long flourish if their leaders are morally compromised, however much they perform piety.
        
- **How his downfall links to larger novel themes**
    
    - Bulstrode’s collapse shows _how hidden histories corrode communal life_ and how personal secrecy becomes a public danger.
        
    - His tragedy complements Dorothea/Lydgate: all three arcs test the interplay between interior life, social appearance, and consequences.
        
    - Eliot asks whether society should forgive practical failings if the public good is served — and tends toward the answer that concealment and hypocrisy are corrosive.

4. Read the Prelude and the Finale and come prepared to discuss them. What is Eliot's aesthetic project, according to these passages?
	- - **Prelude (what it announces about Eliot’s aims)**
    
    - Eliot frames the novel as a social study: she explicitly resists mere romance and insists on a moral-sociological examination of provincial life.
        
    - The Prelude situates the novel in an epistemic humility: the narrator is not omniscient in a godly, absolute sense; the narrative seeks to _sympathetically_ interpret motives and consequences, aware of complexity.
        
    - Eliot’s project is both _didactic and humane_: she wants to instruct (about social justice, responsibility, political life) but to do so without shrill moralising; she practices adult moral discernment.
        
- **Finale (what it shows Eliot doing with those aims)**
    
    - The finale returns to the question of what counts as “success”: domestic steadiness, moral repair, and communal flourishing are valued over heroic genius.
        
    - Eliot’s closure emphasises _slow, partially ambivalent moral progress_: some characters find fulfilment (Mary & Fred), some continue in constrained dignity (Dorothea’s later life), some are punished (Bulstrode), and some are tragically compromised (Lydgate). The world is not neatly moralised, but the author does argue for human responsibility and practical goodness.
        
    - The Finale insists on the moral agency of ordinary persons and the social effects of personal choices — the novel’s ethical center is civic-mindedness and humility rather than aesthetics or fame.
        
- **Overall aesthetic project as a short formula**
    
    - _Middlemarch_ is a realist moral sociology: Eliot attempts to render the texture of social life in all its mundane politics and private longings, to show how character is formed through institutions, economics, and interpersonal obligations, and to insist that moral wisdom is practical, patient, and collective rather than heroic and solitary.
