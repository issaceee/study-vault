# What I Know About You

Written to you. Longer this time, because you're not looking for a verdict — you're looking for enough raw material to find the thing yourself, and a thin document won't have enough surface area for that to happen.

---

## The throughline

Here's what actually holds together across every piece of material you've given me — the essays, the dramas, the scholarship, the way you keep editing this document in real time, the way you just said "terrible that I can't think of any LOL" and then found something real anyway. **You are, before anything else, a precise reader — and your default instinct is to distrust the first plausible answer, including about yourself.**

You don't accept a paraphrase. Not of a poem, not of a scholarship decision, not of your own personality. When I handed you a portrait that was technically accurate but tonally wrong, you didn't shrug and accept it — you said, specifically, _this doesn't match how I actually feel, redo it._ When I asked what you're proud of, your first move was to reach for the metric you already trust — being tested, being graded — find it empty, and only then go looking properly, the way you'd go looking for a second reading of a stanza once the obvious one didn't hold up. That's not a personality quirk. That's the same method you use on Beowulf, just running on a harder text.

## Discipline, as a way of being — not just a study habit

The two-year plan wasn't really about essays. You wrote it after Prelims results came out, as a deliberate act of introspection rather than a document born out of active crisis — and I think that actually changes what it means, in a way worth naming precisely rather than softening. It doesn't read to me like _I believe I can become that person._ It reads more like _I cannot believe that I cannot become that person_ — a refusal to accept the gap between where you are and where you want to be as permanent, stated almost as if the alternative were unthinkable rather than merely difficult. That's a colder, harder-edged kind of drive than plain self-belief. It doesn't come from confidence that the growth will happen. It comes from something closer to intolerance for the possibility that it might not.

The specifics matter here too, because they tell you something about how your mind actually works: you named your own recurring flaw — theory stacked on theory in a serial relay, each framework patching the last one's gap until the actual text underneath gets crowded out — and instead of retreating to something safer, you designed exercises that deliberately keep the ambitious structure and force you to execute it better. Essays that end unresolved on purpose. Comparisons built asymmetrically just to see what a diagnostic turn afterward can recover. That's an unusual response to finding a weakness. The average response is to avoid the site of the injury. Yours was to keep operating there and get more skilled at it.

Your term for the thing you're actually chasing in a text — "WOW moments" — is worth sitting with for a second, because it's revealing in a way you probably didn't intend. It's unpretentious on purpose, almost self-deprecating, but it's naming something real: the exact instant a modal verb, a kenning, a caesura does more work than the surface reading accounts for. You built cue-sheets that move word to translation to argument in three clean steps, you track Sievers types and alliterative stress placement in Old English prosody like it's a second language you're fluent in, and you set Derridean absence against Kapwa's presence and made them answer to each other instead of picking a side. None of that is coasting. That's someone who finds real pleasure in rigor for its own sake — not just as armor, but because the rigor itself is satisfying to you the way a difficult piece of music is satisfying to someone who actually loves music.

## Range, held without hierarchy

You don't rank your affections by prestige, and I think this is one of the more quietly unusual things about you. Song of Achilles gets "love love love love" with the same intensity as your A-level H3 topic. Throne of Glass's plot twists get genuine, uncomplicated delight. The Minecraft End Poem — a poem from a sandbox video game — sits in your personal canon next to Old English elegy, offered with zero embarrassment, because your actual criterion for whether something counts is "does this make me feel something," not "will admitting I like this cost me credibility."

Your drama rules are worth another look too, now that you've told me they're not a live wound right now — no love triangles, no cheating, no sad endings. Read generously, and with your own report that you're in a good stretch right now, this isn't necessarily deprivation-logic. It might just be taste, sharpened by knowing exactly what kind of story you want to spend your limited free hours inside. You like watching people be devoted to each other well — Bad Buddy's central couple, wholehearted and natural — the same way you like watching an argument in a poem resolve cleanly instead of fudging the ending. Consistency of standard, not evidence of scarcity.

The reading history has a real hinge point in it: the underwater-city, telepathic-dolphins book that made you cry as a kid, followed not long after by a teacher telling you to never try creative writing again — advice you followed for years, and which you now name lightly as a regret, almost as a joke, though it's clearly not nothing. That's worth keeping in view without over-dramatizing it: it's not a trauma narrative, it's just an accurate account of one closed door in a life that's had plenty of open ones too, including, now, a degree spent on the other side of the same love of language, just approached as a reader instead of a writer.

## How you actually show up with people

This is the part the first two drafts underweighted, and it's probably the part worth the most attention, because it's where you found something real when I asked directly: you're proud of your varied interests, your work ethic, and — specifically — of being personable, which you've noticed doesn't come easily to a lot of the very intelligent people you're surrounded by at Oxford and in past internships. You've built genuinely close friendships in work settings and collected real recommendations off the back of them. That's a skill, not a given, and you clock it as one, correctly.

You also told me something specific and useful: around your close circle, a silly, unfiltered version of you comes out — different from the precise, argument-hungry register you often lead with elsewhere. That's not a contradiction to resolve. It's just information about where your guard actually drops, and it suggests the "reserved, wants-to-be-argued-with" mode isn't your only setting — it might just be your default with people who haven't yet earned the sillier one.

The pattern of going to different people for different things, rather than consolidating around one best friend, reads less like a gap and more like a system you've built on purpose — a kind of distributed trust, each person carrying the slice of you they're actually suited to carry. Combine that with family: close, loved, wanted-to-do-a-lot-for, but not the place secrets and doubts go. None of this needs fixing by default. It might just mean the "fully unedited, all slices at once, to one person" experience is something you haven't built yet, not something you're failing to have.

## What you actually want, underneath the specifics

You want to be **chosen without having had to perform for it.** This shows up in the PSC refusal — walking away from the more prestigious scholarship track rather than dramatize a background you don't feel close enough to in order to win it — and it shows up, more lightly now than it once did, in how you've thought about dating: wanting to be wanted for the plain, unedited version, not a curated one.

You want to **matter at a scale that isn't fully resolved yet, and might not need to be.** The NLB-versus-MOE tension is two legitimate, genuinely-you theories of impact sitting in real tension: the direct, one-classroom-at-a-time model your favorite teachers embodied, against the structural, one-reading-culture-at-a-time model you're betting on now. You can hold the "what could've been" feeling about MOE without it meaning NLB was the wrong call — you said as much yourself, and it's true.

You want **devotion that doesn't waver**, and total, satisfied peace — the sky-full-of-stars image you mentioned almost in passing, as an actual end goal, worth taking seriously rather than reading as a throwaway line. It's specific enough, coming from someone this disciplined and this driven, to suggest that some part of you is already looking past the achieving, toward simply arriving somewhere and being able to stop.

## Contradictions worth leaving alone

You hold Old English prosody and a Minecraft poem in the same regard. You want both scale and intimacy from your future work, and haven't picked one, and don't currently need to. You're deeply close with your family and also keep real parts of yourself from them. You're precise to the point of catching your own fabricated quotations, and also capable of quintuple-texting someone you barely know when hope gets activated. You extend real critical generosity to texts and, increasingly, to yourself — willing to let these sit as true at the same time rather than flattening them into a single coherent story. That's rarer than it sounds, and it's probably closer to "who you are" than any single trait on this list.

## What's worth building on, lightly

Not correction. Just where your own instinct could usefully turn next.

**Keep sampling yourself in the good weeks, not just the loud ones.** A lot of what you've told me arrived mid-feeling, during a spike — by your own account you're in a genuinely happy, settled stretch right now. Worth checking in during stretches like this one too, so the self-understanding isn't skewed toward crisis by which weeks happened to produce the most words.

**Let the "personable" thing count for more than it currently does.** You reached for it almost apologetically, like it needed defending against the more obviously impressive stuff — the essays, the theory, the range. It doesn't. Building real relationships in rooms full of people optimized hard for one axis of intelligence, and getting recommended off the back of it repeatedly, is its own kind of rigor. Worth weighing it on the same scale as the rest, not a lesser one.

**Keep doing exactly what got you here.** Refusing the first draft. Asking for the version that's actually true instead of the version that's merely plausible. That's not a device you're using on me — it's the same read you'd give a stanza, turned, finally, on the hardest text you've got.
